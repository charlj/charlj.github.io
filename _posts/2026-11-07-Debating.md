Discussions, debates and arguments can become quite chaotic. Patience, humility and an openness to hear another's views are required for a constructive discussion. Any discussion where views differ is costly. One engages out of interest, to learn or to win. The motivation for why we engage others who hold a different view matters for the way we go about the discussion. Everyone has a tactic. Some are respectful, some give in to emotional responses, some become outright outraged.

This post is about one particular failure: intelligent people arguing badly. Intelligence does not protect us from bias. Often it makes the bias harder to dislodge, because a clever mind is better at defending what it already believes. I start with two examples from my own conversations, then name the biases at work, and then use a little Bayesian arithmetic to show why some worldviews do not move no matter how much evidence you put in front of them. I end with what we can do about it, starting with ourselves.

## Two examples

The last couple of weeks I was listening to debates about salvation between Protestants and Catholics. Boy, did those debates heat up. I could not help noticing how seemingly intelligent people got riled up. People veered off the topic and into ad hominem attacks that were very personal.

### Afrikaner refugees

Recently I had a discussion with a very intelligent friend (well read and with plenty of experience of life). I will provide some context, but the topic itself is not that important for this entry. The topic is whether Afrikaners should take up the US's refugee program. The program offers Afrikaners refugee status in the US on the grounds that they face persecution in South Africa. In most cases, though not all, it excludes non-Afrikaners. It is a heated topic because some claim there is a white genocide in South Africa. Make what you will of whether the program is good or bad and whether it is justified. Should an Afrikaner take up this chance? My personal view is that I do not have a problem with it, especially if it helps the family that becomes refugees. I should declare a stake: a close family member took up the program. That gives me a reason to see it kindly, and it is worth keeping in mind as you read. An Afrikaner may choose this option for several reasons. They range from a personal negative experience in South Africa (being a victim of crime, psychological duress), to feeling excluded or discriminated against, to simple opportunism. Who truly knows the heart and motivation of every person?

Unfortunately many people label these Afrikaners as traitors. Traitors to what? The government made some very nasty remarks about these people, many of whom were victims of awful crimes. What were my friend's objections to Afrikaner refugees? (i) They do not have solidarity with the rest of suffering South Africans, (ii) they are selfish, and (iii) they perpetuate a bad and false image of South Africa. Ultimately they are traitors, to what I don't know.

At face value, one could have a conversation around these points. One would have to prove a few things and substantiate some pretty heroic assumptions. Fine. However, the conversation became heated, and the points were made with emotional force and from a moral vantage point. Because I disagreed, my responses were met with "how can someone as well read as you not see this?", or with glee when parts of the program turned out to have holes (some bad Afrikaners came into the country), or the discussion turned personal with rapid-fire questions about my own leaving South Africa. When I offered a rebuttal I was often told "but you are just simply wrong".

The case I tried to make, calmly, was about what solidarity means. For my friend, solidarity requires staying and sharing the burden. For me, solidarity is about how you treat and support people, and one can do that from another country. On my definition, a family that leaves does not harm those who stay, and if South Africa treated Afrikaners better, few would take up the offer. Notice that we were not really disagreeing about facts. We were disagreeing about the meaning of a word, and we never stopped to define it.

To be fair to my friend, his third point is the one that could be tested: if refugee claims damage South Africa's image, that should show up somewhere, in investment or tourism, say. But here the economist in me asks about the marginal effect. South Africa does far more damage to its own image than any refugee program could. Unemployment is high and rising, GDP per capita has stagnated or declined for more than a decade, infrastructure is eroding, and crime is extremely high. Investors react to those things. Against that backdrop, the marginal effect of the program on the economy is probably very small (perhaps not zero), and stopping it is not what will help the country. My own claim is an empirical claim too, and I should hold it the same way I ask my friend to hold his. If it turned out that the program measurably damaged South Africa's standing, or drained skills the country cannot replace, I would have to revise my view.

Whether I was right or wrong is not really the topic of this entry. The topic is the tactics used to out-wrestle the other person. The same tactics play out on a much larger stage. I did not understand some of the US left-leaning folks who smirked at and celebrated the death of someone like Charlie Kirk. Honestly, I found it quite disturbing, regardless of whether I agreed or disagreed with his views. Many people lose the ability to be civil in discussion and debate.

### Climate change

Despite being presented with evidence, some people cannot shift their views. I do not mean to pick on my friend, but here is a second example from our conversations. Climate change is real. But I cannot predict precipitation and temperature changes 1 to 30 years from now. The best climate models have to assume economic pathways (growth, population change, energy intensity, energy technologies), and each pathway maps onto different precipitation and temperature changes. Climate models are scenario based (conditional on these assumptions) and probabilistic: we cannot know with certainty how the climate will evolve. There is a majority view on the likely range of warming, but by no means is every climate scientist in the middle of it. Then, probably even harder to predict, is how temperature and precipitation map onto climate damages (floods, heat, droughts). We have models of these things too. We cannot know the outcomes for certain. That said, we should not be idle and do nothing. We should be concerned.

My friend, who means well, argues that the world is doomed and that we are past a tipping point (a threshold beyond which warming feeds on itself and cannot be reversed). His evidence is the Guardian. A newspaper is a filter: it reports the most alarming findings, so its headlines carry less weight than the source material behind them, which is usually more cautious. I should be careful here too, because I do not know exactly what he read. I worked on climate change for many years, and I can tell you that we do not know whether climate change is the most important risk in the world. Other risks may or may not matter more (asteroids, nuclear war, pandemics, the Terminator, aliens), each with some probability of occurring. These are called existential risks. Some, like asteroids, we understand reasonably well. Others are close to unknown unknowns. We have never witnessed humanity being wiped out by any of them, but we can conceive of it happening. If they have a non-zero probability of happening, then we should have policies to reduce them as much as we can. The problem with the debate, again, is not whether these things may happen. It is the moral position that one risk is more important than another, and that if the other person does not see it that way, then that person is just wrong. Note how a moral element always slips into the debate.

None of this makes me immune. I have a family stake in the first example and a professional one in the second. The biases below are ones I need to watch in myself.

In the next section I highlight some biases that all of us need to guard against when we discuss things. I want us to stop using "intelligence" as a means of self-defense, and get back to the idea that our intelligence should be used for truth-seeking.

## Biases to guard against

The uncomfortable finding is that intelligence does not protect against these biases. Dan Kahan and coauthors gave people the same statistical puzzle twice: once framed around a skin cream, once around gun control. On the skin cream, the more numerate people did better. On gun control, the more numerate people did better only when the correct answer suited their politics. The most numerate partisans ended up the furthest apart ("Motivated numeracy and enlightened self-government", 2017). Skill at reasoning was put to work defending a side.

- **Dysrationalia:** Keith Stanovich's term for when an intelligent person holds onto irrational beliefs despite possessing the cognitive tools to know better.
- **Confirmation bias:** Smart people are skilled at weaponizing logic, defending pre-existing conclusions rather than testing them.
- **Amygdala hijack:** When a debate feels like an attack on one's identity, the brain triggers a fight-or-flight response. The term is pop-science shorthand, but the core is real: under threat, stress hormones (cortisol and adrenaline) rise and the slow, deliberate reasoning of the prefrontal cortex suffers.
- **Emotional truth traps:** Confusing how strongly something feels right with what actually is right (e.g., if something aligns with my worldview, it must be right).
- **Belief perseverance:** Beliefs survive even after the evidence that formed them has been discredited. A stronger claim, the backfire effect, says that correcting someone makes them believe the falsehood more firmly. Large studies find the backfire effect is rare. The usual result is not that evidence backfires, but that it barely moves people. That is the rigid prior I come to below.
- **Identity threat & tribalism:** Smart people often have an ego that is tied to being right or informed. When someone challenges them, they perceive it as a threat to who they are, and the intellectual debate becomes an existential battle.
- **Overconfidence beyond one's field:** Strictly, the Dunning-Kruger effect is about the unskilled overrating themselves. The version that afflicts intelligent people is different: expertise in one field breeds confidence in another. They overstate the breadth of their knowledge and dismiss counterarguments out of hand.

When an argument becomes intensely emotional, discussions can devolve into (i) ad hominem attacks (becoming personal, focusing on someone's tone, or challenging someone's intelligence), (ii) straw men (distorting or exaggerating an opponent's point into something that is easy to defeat), and (iii) red herrings (deflecting the core question into an abstract or tangential argument).

## On Bayesian updating

We all hold beliefs about almost anything we are presented with. Some of those beliefs are stronger than others. The beliefs we hold before we see new evidence are called our priors. One person might truly believe that the world is doomed and that climate change is past a tipping point. A challenger might say that humans contribute very little to climate change. Yet others hold an uninformative prior and simply say that they do not know.

Bayes' rule tells us how a rational person should revise a belief when new evidence arrives. Let $H$ be a hypothesis, say "humans are a significant cause of current warming", and let $E$ be a piece of evidence. Write $\neg H$ for "$H$ is false". Then

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)\,P(\neg H)}$$

$P(H)$ is the prior. $P(E \mid H)$ is the likelihood: how probable the evidence would be if $H$ were true. $P(H \mid E)$ is the posterior, the updated belief. The rule is easier to read in odds:

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(\neg H \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid \neg H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}}$$

The likelihood ratio measures how strong the evidence is. A ratio of 3 means the evidence is three times as likely if $H$ is true as if it is false. A ratio of 1/3 is equally strong evidence the other way. A ratio of 1 means the evidence tells you nothing. Each new (independent) piece of evidence multiplies the odds again. Take logs and belief becomes a running sum:

$$\log \frac{P(H \mid E_1,\dots,E_n)}{P(\neg H \mid E_1,\dots,E_n)} = \log \frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \frac{P(E_i \mid H)}{P(E_i \mid \neg H)}$$

Where you end up is where you started, plus the weight of the evidence. A fully rational person updates their priors when presented with evidence. Say you have data that clearly shows humanity's contribution to climate change is significant. The person who denied the role of humans will update her priors.

But how far she moves depends on where she starts. Take two people and a claim $H$. Ana is unsure, with a prior of 0.5. Ben is nearly certain, with a prior of 0.99. Both see the same three independent pieces of evidence against $H$, each with a likelihood ratio of 1/3.

| Pieces of evidence seen | Ana's belief in $H$ | Ben's belief in $H$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 0 | 0.50 | 0.99 |
| 1 | 0.25 | 0.97 |
| 2 | 0.10 | 0.92 |
| 3 | 0.04 | 0.79 |

After three pieces of evidence Ana has all but abandoned $H$. Ben still holds it with 79% confidence, and he needs five pieces before he believes $H$ is more likely false than true. Note that Ben is not being irrational. He is slow. A strong prior can be earned, and it is reasonable that one study does not overturn a lifetime of reading. The trouble starts at the extremes.

**The first problem is a prior of exactly 0 or 1.** If $P(H) = 1$, then $P(\neg H) = 0$, the prior odds are infinite, and the posterior equals 1 whatever the evidence. No finite likelihood ratio moves it. Statisticians call the rule against such priors Cromwell's rule (the name is Dennis Lindley's), after Oliver Cromwell's plea to the Church of Scotland in 1650: "I beseech you, in the bowels of Christ, think it possible you may be mistaken."

Yet, in many debates we do not see truth-seeking as the ultimate objective. The debate is infused with tactics to win. The debater's priors are hyper-rigid. It looks like no amount of evidence shifts this person's worldview.

I see this in economic debates. Take the classic arguments about communism vs. capitalism. The crazy part, to me, is that the precepts of these theories are testable. Many communists take on a moral mantle for their position, and because "morality" is at stake they dismiss the counterexample out of hand (even against strong evidence).

Moral foundations become "sacred values" (see the work of Philip Tetlock). Sacred values are non-negotiable. In the language above, a sacred value is a prior set to 1. The moment you bring empirical evidence or cost-benefit analysis to a sacred value, the person experiences it as an insult. Tetlock calls this a taboo trade-off. Instead of updating their prior, an emotional debater experiences moral outrage. They view the mere request to look at counter-evidence as a sign of corruption or bad faith on your part.

**The second problem is subtler, and it is the one clever people are best at.** Few people will admit to a prior of exactly 1. It is much easier to attack the likelihood. "Your source is biased." "That study was funded by the wrong people." "How can someone as well read as you not see this?" Each of these says that the evidence would have shown up whether or not $H$ is true, so that $P(E \mid H) = P(E \mid \neg H)$. The likelihood ratio becomes 1, its log becomes 0, and the evidence adds nothing to the sum. Once the messenger is cast as acting in bad faith, every piece of evidence he brings is set to zero weight. It can go further. If counter-evidence is read as proof of the other side's corruption, evidence against $H$ ends up raising belief in $H$. Two people can then look at the same data and move further apart.

This is why smart people can be slower to update, not faster. They will have spent countless hours justifying their beliefs and finding ways to articulate them, and those hours buy them a better supply of reasons to discount whatever evidence they are shown. A skilled debater who is biased may very well win an argument against an unskilled debater who is objectively right.

For me it is hard to debate someone who has a self-righteous bias, or someone who engages in moral grandstanding (Justin Tosi and Brandon Warmke's term for using moral talk to raise one's own standing). If someone takes a morally superior view on a subjective topic, they unlock psychological rewards: ego inflation (it feels good to be the "good person"), exemption from nuance (if my position is morally pure, then yours is morally compromised, which means I don't have to listen to your data), and naive realism (they believe they see the world objectively as it is, so others who disagree must be unintelligent, uneducated or acting out of malice). Each of these rewards is a reason to keep the prior at 1 and the likelihood ratio at 1.

## So, how do we counter?

Sometimes, if you know a person reacts this way, it is better to disengage. You will not achieve much. However, you are not left in the dark. You have ways to get to the truth (and you need to be aware that all of us have these biases, so check yourself too). Some of the ideas below lower an overconfident prior. Others restore the weight of the evidence.

- **Reframe from why into how:** Ask the person to explain *how* their solution would work, step by step. When people are forced to explain the mechanics of their belief, they can be confronted by how shallow that belief is. Psychologists call this the illusion of explanatory depth. In experiments by Philip Fernbach and coauthors, asking people to explain how a policy works moderated their positions, while asking them to give reasons for it did not. This can lower their confidence in their prior.
- **Affirm their identity:** Validate their intelligence and moral intent before the debate. When people feel respected and secure in their identity, they may be more open to counterarguments.
- **Use identity-safe framing:** Frame your counter-evidence in their moral language and values.
- **Confidence calibration:** Ask people to assign a probability to the confidence they place in a statement. Once they give an estimate, ask what evidence they would need to drop their confidence below a certain threshold. If no amount of evidence could shift their view, they have just told you their prior is 1, and you know they are holding onto dogma.
- **Ideological Turing test:** Bryan Caplan's idea. Propose a game: I will argue your perspective and you try to counter me, and thereafter you argue my perspective and I try to counter you. If neither of us can state the other's view in a way the other accepts, we are not yet disagreeing about the same thing.
- **Reductio ad absurdum (taking priors to their logical limit):** If someone has rigid priors, take them at face value and ask about the consequences. For example, if humans are the main contributors to climate change, what does this imply for growth, population change, energy usage and taxes, and what does it mean for you at a personal level?
- **Operationalize definitions:** Strip away abstract words. Turn *the US harms South Africa with the refugee policy* into a claim where the harm is identified and has a metric.
- **Co-author:** Present the data as a mutual problem to solve. This shifts the debate from a zero-sum game to a cooperative game.
- **Call it out:** If someone strawmans an argument, pause the debate, point it out in a friendly manner, and ask why the response is hostile.

## The end goal should be harmony

We often debate people because we care. We think we have evidence that a current worldview is damaging. We need to test those views, be open about the nuances, and be able to update our priors in the face of new evidence. In the language of this post: never set a prior to 0 or 1, and never set the weight of someone's evidence to zero because you dislike the messenger. See your debater as acting in good faith. Seek truth at all cost, not the desire to be right (even though that desire might be strong).

Be Christ-like in your approach with others. Be kind, be merciful, be patient, be generous, be fair and love your neighbor. If you are wrong, acknowledge it and thank the person for helping you correct an error. If the topic is too subjective and the evidence is fuzzy, acknowledge that before arguing.
