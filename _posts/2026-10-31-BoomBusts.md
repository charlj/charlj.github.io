How does the model of the soul work in our current environment? In the [previous posts](/posts/2026/07/SoulEcon3/) I built the model and its propositions. In this post I put it to work on an old fight in economics: when the economy booms and busts, whom should we trust, the market or the state?

Everyone has an ideology that presupposes a solution to inequality and poverty. Most people mean well, but sometimes their economics do not work out so well. Of course I am very ideological too. The debate, one that has been running for several decades, is whether untamed individual freedom leads to greed that harms people, so that those who amassed so much should redistribute their resources to those less fortunate. This is the story of the Marxists and, in a milder form, the Post-Keynesians, who both argue that capitalists capture what workers produce. (Post-Keynesians are not necessarily Marxists, but both put labor at the center: for Marx as the source of value, for the Post-Keynesians as the source of production and demand.) The belief, or the stated claim, makes them feel justified that the wealthy should be taxed more (see recent debates in California). The logic is the same as taxing an externality: if exploitation is a harm, then put a price on the harm. Essentially this means greater state control.

Free market folks put more emphasis on individual liberty. Invest in education, save by being patient, and the present value of the returns that accrue to you will be higher than if you did not do those things. Greater government control infringes on that liberty by taxing your decisions and then deciding what to do with the money it collects. The government is made up of individuals who may or may not be moral agents. Those who hold onto liberty would much rather make their own choices than have the government make them on their behalf.

The fight is sharpest when the economy breaks. A crisis tests each side's story about human nature, so that is where I want to look.

## Boom-bust cycles

There are many papers about what creates crises. Demand and supply shocks are not the same (a crisis engineered by governments, such as during Covid-19, is different from the global financial crisis). Let's try to predict how the next crisis will unfold.

Currently the S&P 500 is rising faster than the economy is growing. Most of the stock market is driven by the big tech companies. These companies are expanding operations, building data centers, improving AI and bidding for inputs (rare earths, chips, power). The expansion in turn raises demand for more compute. Most of the AI companies are not yet profitable, but the idea is that future demand will justify the current expansion. If that proves to be true, then what we see in the stock market is perfectly rational.

However, Hayek offers us something different to think about. If credit is cheap (interest rates are below the natural rate, the rate that would match real saving to investment), then entrepreneurs make decisions based on a false signal. They make long-term capital investments that look profitable but are not backed by real consumer saving. As these projects progress, they compete for resources and drive up the prices of material inputs and wages. When the economy realizes that saving is insufficient to pay for these projects, a correction takes place: projects are not completed, unemployment increases and output falls.

At the core of Hayek's argument is that rates sit below the natural rate because credit grows faster than saving. Printing money, or banks lending money that nobody saved, pushes the market rate down.

An obvious objection is that entrepreneurs should learn. If cheap credit fools them in every cycle, why do they keep falling for it? The modern answer is that attention is costly. A price carries a noisy signal, part real demand and part cheap credit, and separating the two takes effort that most agents rationally do not spend (Lucas, 1972, on noisy signals; Sims, 2003, on rational inattention). Agents do not have perfect foresight. Being fooled sometimes is not irrational. It is the price of not watching everything.

For Keynes, booms are driven by *animal spirits*: the optimism and herd behavior of investors that lead to periods of high investment. Busts occur when sudden bouts of pessimism lower the expected profits on capital and investment declines. Recessions and unemployment can last for long periods because wages and prices are sticky and do not adjust to the collapse in spending. Keynes argued that when a recession occurs we should spend more, or rather, the government should spend more. For Hayek this is adding fuel to the fire, since debt is at the heart of the problem. For Keynes it just means lifting aggregate demand back up to where supply is.

Then come the Post-Keynesians, with Minsky, who argue that instability is a feature of capitalism: stability itself breeds instability. The *financial instability hypothesis* says that during booms managers become increasingly confident and consequently take on more risk and more debt. Three phases describe the hypothesis: (i) hedge finance, where borrowers cover both principal and interest from cash flow $\rightarrow$ (ii) speculative finance, where cash flow covers interest but the principal is rolled over $\rightarrow$ (iii) Ponzi finance, where cash flow does not even cover interest, and borrowers rely on rising asset prices to borrow more or sell assets to pay interest. The *Minsky moment* is a rapid, self-reinforcing bust triggered by something like an interest rate increase or a missed revenue projection, which forces borrowers to sell assets. Panic ensues as similarly leveraged borrowers sell, which drives prices down even more. A liquidity squeeze follows: lenders pull back, financing dries up, and previously viable companies go bankrupt.

Notice that Minsky and Hayek describe the same boom. Debt piles up, asset prices run ahead of cash flows, and the bust comes when the financing stops. What divides them is the remedy. Hayek wants the credit stopped before it starts. Minsky wants a big government and a big central bank to catch the fall.

Marxists interpret boom-bust cycles as part of the DNA of capitalism, driven by the quest for profits. For Marxists, recessions are not caused by mood (Keynes), overconfidence (Minsky) or debt (Hayek), but by *the tendency of the rate of profit to fall*. Booms are driven by fierce competition among capitalists, who invest heavily in machinery, automation and technology. For Marx this is a contradiction: human labor is the source of surplus value, so replacing workers with machines across the economy lowers the rate of profit. Capitalists try to offset falling profits by producing more, but workers whose wages are kept low by competition (exploitation) cannot afford to buy the goods, leading to a crisis of overproduction and underconsumption. The crisis is a reset. Excess capital is destroyed, wages are driven down, and profit margins recover enough for the cycle to repeat.

Monetarists believe that business cycles come from mismanaging the growth of the money supply. Booms happen when the central bank grows the money supply faster than the economy grows. Recessions happen when money growth stops or the money supply shrinks. Friedman, the leading monetarist, argued (with Anna Schwartz) that the Great Depression was not a failure of capitalism but a failure of the Federal Reserve, which let the money supply collapse.

Schumpeter's **creative destruction** treats booms and busts as part of economic progress, driven by waves of innovation that continuously destroy old economic structures from within. Booms begin when a radical entrepreneur introduces a groundbreaking technology (e.g., railroads, automobiles, the internet, AI), which brings with it new investment, new businesses and economic euphoria. The bust comes when the boom saturates the market. The technology becomes common, and profit margins vanish. Inefficient businesses are forced to scale down or go bankrupt.

Finally, the **New Classical** view, in its Real Business Cycle (RBC) form, is that booms and busts are not market failures but the economy's optimal, rational response to real shocks. Booms are driven by positive supply shocks (e.g., a new innovation), which make labor and capital more productive. Rational investors invest more and rational workers work more (profits and wages are higher). Busts follow negative supply shocks (e.g., supply chain disruptions or regulatory burdens), which lower productivity. Real wages drop, rational workers substitute leisure for work, firms hold back on investment (or hoard cash), and the economy contracts.

Seven schools are a lot to hold in one's head. I would group them into three families.

1. **Monetary and credit family:** Hayek and the Monetarists both locate the cause in money: changing its supply alters the price of borrowing. Both distrust discretion. Hayek would let markets clear, and Friedman would bind the central bank to a rule. There is of course nuance, but I think the difference between too much money and too-cheap loans is not that great. Large amounts of money always end up somewhere.
2. **Supply side and innovation family:** Schumpeter and the RBC economists put innovation at the center of economic life. A boom here is not an illusion. It is a productivity leap.
3. **Endogenous instability family:** Keynes, Minsky and Marx believe that capitalism contains the seeds of its own instability and requires intervention in order to survive. *Animal spirits, overconfidence and fierce competition* are, to me, closely connected. For Marx, the fierce competition is not necessarily driven by greed, but by the fear of being crushed by competitors. On the surface, this survival instinct looks much like Keynes's herd behavior or Minsky's financial euphoria.

So what is the AI boom? Each family reads it differently. The innovation family says the boom is real: AI is a productivity leap and the capital follows it. The credit family asks whether the spending is backed by saving or by cheap money, and watches the input prices (chips, power, electricians) that the projects bid up. The instability family watches the debt, and asks whether the financing is sliding from cash flow towards borrowing against rising valuations. My guess is that all three are partly right at the same time, and that is what makes the next correction hard to call.

## A story of belief

Look again at the table. Animal spirits, confidence, competition, innovation, trust in the government, trust in liberty: every school ends up telling a story about what people believe. Even the tools of macroeconomics are tools for changing minds. Central banks target inflation mostly by shaping what people expect it to be. In 2012 Mario Draghi said the European Central Bank would do "whatever it takes" to save the euro, and bond spreads fell before the bank bought a single bond. A sentence did the work of a bailout. Advertising works on the same margin. Galbraith called it the dependence effect: production creates the wants it then claims to satisfy.

The words carry it too. Credit comes from the Latin *credere*, to believe. A fiduciary is someone we have faith (*fides*) in. Fiat money takes its name from the Latin "let it be", the word God speaks in Genesis 1:3 (*fiat lux*). A piece of paper is worth something because we believe others will accept it, and they accept it because they believe the same of us.

I should not overstate this. Belief does not give value to everything. A loaf of bread feeds me whether or not anyone else believes in it. Belief matters in proportion to how far away the payoff is. Bread pays today. A data center pays over a decade. Money pays only when someone else accepts it. The further away the payoff, the more the price becomes a belief about other people's beliefs, which is Keynes's beauty contest: investors judge not which face is prettiest, but which face others will think prettiest. That is why booms gather in long-dated assets, exactly where Hayek's malinvestment sits.

A belief that rests only on other beliefs is fragile. Once a few doubt, everyone has a reason to doubt, and that is a bust. A belief anchored in something outside the market (a real cash flow, an honest balance sheet, a central bank that keeps its word) survives a change in mood. This is why grace in the model is exogenous. Faith, in the Christian telling, is not a belief we talk ourselves into because others hold it. It is a gift that comes from outside the system (Ephesians 2:8). A high soul state lets a person hold beliefs that do not move with the crowd.

## Elements of truth, but missing the point

Personally, I think all of these schools contain elements of truth. There is a reason many well-meaning people can be grouped into each of these families. It is true that some people are greedy and would seek profit at the expense of others. However, not all people are so greedy that they would hurt others. It is true that some people operate out of fear, and crush their opponents to avoid the pain that fear causes. However, not all people are driven by fear. Some people are just curious, like creating things and enjoy a bit of competition. It is true that some people seek euphoria and love taking risks. However, some people are more careful when making decisions.

Can capitalists simply be grouped as the bad guys who exploit others? Or can they simply be grouped as the great innovators who steer the world? What is the environment that incentivizes bad versus good actions? What is behind each agent's decision to invest, exploit, take care of others, be fair or steal?

The model of the soul from the previous posts tries to get to the *bottom* of our decisions. Each family's mechanism runs through a parameter in the model.

Take the credit family first. Hayek's false signal is a gap between the saving that prices imply and the saving that exists. In the model, patience $\beta(S_t)$ rises with the soul state. A patient society actually saves, so a low interest rate is more likely to be a true signal than a false one. Satiation $\sigma(S_t)$ works on the other side of the loan. Credit can be contained if we are more easily satisfied with what we have, and a satisfied person is harder to sell what they do not need.

Now the instability family. Minsky's slide from hedge to Ponzi finance is not only overconfidence. Ponzi finance needs someone to overstate cash flows, or to look away when they are overstated. That is the honesty condition from [pt.3](/posts/2026/07/SoulEcon3/): the agent cheats when the rents on offer, $\lambda_t \Pi_t$, are worth more than the soul that cheating damages, $\mu^S_t \rho$. Dishonesty does something worse than enrich the cheat. It adds noise to prices. Hayek's price system carries information only as well as the people posting the prices tell the truth, so a low soul state makes the inattentive agent easier to fool.

The innovation family is the case the soul should not fix. Schumpeter's booms are real, and creative destruction is how progress happens. What the soul changes is who carries the losers. Generosity $\psi(S_t)$ makes those who do not have the responsibility of individuals and communities, and not of a government that is also made up of broken people.

I should be careful not to claim too much. A high soul state does not abolish the cycle. Shocks still arrive, attention is still costly, and good people still misread noisy prices. What it changes is the size of the swing: less credit chasing saving that does not exist, fewer Ponzi borrowers, and a softer landing for those who lose.

It also explains why the balance keeps swinging between the market and the state. In a low soul state, markets breed greed, greed breeds a bust, and the bust breeds calls for the state. But the state is staffed by the same low-soul agents, so its discretion is captured and corrupted, and the calls swing back to the market. Neither side repairs the variable that is broken. We keep optimizing over the wrong things.

Keynes saw this. In 1944 he wrote to Hayek that he agreed with *The Road to Serfdom* morally and philosophically, but that moderate planning "will be safe if those carrying it out are rightly orientated in their own minds and hearts to the moral issue." Hayek's market needs honest prices. Keynes's planner needs honest officials. Both quietly borrow a high soul state that neither system produces.

A low soul state predicts greed, corruption and exploitation, under either system. Ironically, this is where Marx had it wrong. He saw the greed and blamed the liberty. Individual liberty steeped in love for God and love for one's neighbor solves redistribution, and it also eliminates the free-riding of bad individuals that no state can police. Burke put it plainly in 1791: men are qualified for civil liberty "in exact proportion to their disposition to put moral chains upon their own appetites." The less we restrain our own appetites, the more the state will restrain them for us.
